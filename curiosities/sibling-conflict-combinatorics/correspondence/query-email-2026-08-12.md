Greetings Professors Parshall and Tabachnikov,

I'd like to ask whether a short article would be a fit for the Intelligencer before submitting it formally.

The piece started at my kitchen table. I have four kids, each two years apart, and I grew up with only one sibling, so the dynamics of a larger family were new to me. What kept striking me was that the number of distinct ways they could get into a fight seemed never-ending, so I set out to count it for my own situation.

- The answer for four children is 25
- The number of ways n children can split into two opposing sides, with others uninvolved, is (3^n − 2·2^n + 1)/2, which turns out to be the Stirling number S(n+1, 3)
- Four kids form just 6 sibling pairs but allow 25 conflict configurations, and by eight children the count reaches 3,025 against 28 pairs

Having the number was useful to me as a parent, and I thought it might be useful to other parents and to researchers, so I went looking for prior work. Both ingredients are well established, coalition structure in game theory on one side and coalition dynamics in family systems research on the other, but I could find nothing in the literature that combines them to put an actual number on the space of possible sibling conflicts for a family of n children. The closest antecedents are Bossard (1945) and Kephart (1950), who counted relationships and subgroups but not opposing sides. The article is explicit that it measures a possibility space, not actual behavior.

It runs about 2,000 words with two figures and one table, written for a general mathematical readership. I write from industry rather than academia. The compiled PDF is attached. I'm happy to submit through the portal if you think it merits review, or to hear that it belongs elsewhere, including the Gems and Curiosities column if that's the better home.
